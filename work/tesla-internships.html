Fremont — scaling material flow

A production ramp of that size does not fail on the big machines; it fails on the hundred small ways material does not get where it needs to be. I delivered 24+ material handling solutions alongside automated data collection systems, each targeting a specific flow constraint on the floor.

Designing for a ramp means designing for a moving target: the layout you build for today's rate has to survive the rate six months out, which pushes every decision toward modularity over point solutions.

Universal robotic end-of-arm tool

Dedicated end-of-arm tooling means a new tool, a new design cycle and new spares for every part family. I prototyped a low-fidelity universal EOAT intended to cover a range of parts with one tool, taking it far enough to prove the concept and cost it out — $300K projected savings over two years.

The engineering question was where to put the flexibility: a tool that adapts to everything grips nothing well. The prototype was about finding the part-family boundary where one tool is still better than three.

Controls integration

I integrated Siemens and Allen-Bradley PLCs across Ethernet and PROFINET — two vendor ecosystems that do not naturally cooperate. The resulting cross-network setup was adopted afterward as a training rig for Controls Technicians, used to teach troubleshooting of cross-network devices such as conveyor VFDs.

That second life is the part I am proudest of: the setup outlived the immediate problem and became infrastructure other people learned on.

Austin — standardization

At the Austin site the work shifted from individual solutions to the system that produces them. I designed mechanical racks and dollies to streamline equipment setup and material flow, then standardized how the team selected and designed equipment — establishing modular, reusable production systems so the next engineer starts from a catalog rather than a blank sheet.
